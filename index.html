Immigration enforcement

ICE's arrests in Maryland nearly doubled this year. The growth is people with no criminal record

By Mehedi Marof · Capital News Service

Also in this series: After the arrest

Scroll to read↓

From January through July of this year, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement arrested nearly 3,000 people in Maryland who had neither a criminal conviction nor a pending criminal charge. That is more than the agency arrested in the state in the nearly three years before, from October 2022 through July 2025, combined.

Total ICE arrests in Maryland nearly doubled, from 2,224 in January through July 2025 to over 4,000 in the same months of 2026. All of that increase came from people without a record. Arrests of people with convictions and of people facing pending charges each fell slightly.

"Maryland feels like a microcosm of what's happening nationally," said Yulie Landan, a staff attorney at the National Immigration Project, a legal group suing ICE over its arrests in the state and over conditions in its Baltimore hold room. "We've been seeing massive increases in arrests there."

Capital News Service analyzed ICE arrest, detainer and detention records that the Deportation Data Project, a research group at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA, obtained through Freedom of Information Act lawsuits. The records run through Aug. 6, 2026.

Maureen Sweeney, who directs the Immigration Clinic at the University of Maryland Francis King Carey School of Law, said she sees "a big disconnect between the administration's rhetoric about ... keeping the country safe from dangerous people and what's actually happening."

What is happening, she said, is "that we're targeting and rounding up anybody who's a non-citizen… especially people who are brown or Black and look different from white Americans."

What "no record" means

ICE sorts the people it arrests by their criminal history at the time of arrest: people previously convicted of a crime, people facing a pending criminal charge and people with neither. ICE calls the third group “other immigration violators.” They are accused of violating civil immigration law, such as entering the country without permission or overstaying a visa, but ICE's systems show no criminal conviction or pending charge. The label does not mean they were in the country legally.

Arrests rose across the country too, but followed different patterns. Nationwide, ICE made nearly 253,000 arrests from January through July, up about 75% from a year earlier.

Arrests of people with no record grew the most nationally as well. But arrests of people with pending charges also rose by nearly two-thirds, and arrests of people with convictions by about a fifth. In Maryland, both of those groups shrank.

The increase was steepest on the Eastern Shore. ICE arrested 730 people with no record in the Shore's nine counties from January through July, nearly four times the 189 it arrested there in the same months of 2025.

Since Trump took office, about 23% of ICE's arrests in Maryland have been on the Eastern Shore, a region home to about 7% of the state's residents.

Wicomico County, which includes Salisbury, had the highest ICE arrest rate in the state, about 107 arrests for every 10,000 residents. Nearly all were made in the community.

ICE also made arrests inside its own offices in Baltimore, including one where immigrants under supervision report for scheduled check-ins. Since Jan. 20, 2025, the agency has recorded 889 arrests at the two offices, about three in four of them of people with no record.

Those office arrests rose from 62 in the first three months of 2025 to 281 in the first three months of 2026, the most in any quarter in the records.

"Maryland was really kind of on the forefront of mass increases in arrests, whether it be in the street or in check-ins," Landan said.

Maryland stands out

Since Trump took office, about 63% of ICE's arrests in Maryland have been of people with no criminal record, compared with about 39% nationwide.

Among the 43 states and territories where ICE made at least 1,000 arrests in that period, only New York and West Virginia had a higher share.

The share has swung widely over the years. In late 2022, under President Joe Biden, about three in four people ICE arrested in Maryland had no record. By December 2024, the share had fallen to about 17%.

In January 2025, the month Trump returned to office, Maryland's share matched the nation's, at about 21%. Every month since, it has been higher than the national share, peaking at about 77% in February 2026, double the national rate that month.

Most people ICE arrested in Maryland are from Central America and Mexico. Since Jan. 20, 2025, about 78% were citizens of Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras or Mexico.

Most of the growth came from non-custodial arrests, those made at homes, workplaces, roadsides and ICE's own offices.

Immigration lawyers say the numbers cut against how the administration describes its enforcement. "They keep saying we're going after the worst of the worst and the reality is they're going after everyone," Landan said. "It's just about the numbers for them."

How we did this analysis

The records

This analysis uses U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement records that the Deportation Data Project, a research group at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA, obtained through Freedom of Information Act lawsuits and published on its data page. We used the project's release of Aug. 24, 2026, which covers Oct. 1, 2022, through Aug. 6, 2026, and four of its files: arrests, detainers, arrests linked to detention stays, and detention stints. In every file, we removed rows the project flags as likely duplicates, using its duplicate_drop_row column. More information about the methodology and the notebook can be found on this github repository

Arrests

In the arrests file, each row is one ICE arrest. We counted an arrest as a Maryland arrest when the project's apprehension_state_filled_in column says Maryland. That column uses the state ICE recorded and, when ICE left it blank, fills it in from the arrest location; it identifies 12,859 Maryland rows, 214 more than ICE's own state field. After removing 218 likely duplicates, 12,641 arrests remained. Some people were arrested more than once, and 28 rows have no person identifier (unique_identifier), so the story counts arrests rather than people.

Criminal history comes from apprehension_criminality, which records what ICE's systems showed at the time of arrest: "Convicted Criminal," "Pending Criminal Charges" or "Other Immigration Violator." The story calls the last group people with no prior conviction or pending charge. Whether a person already had a final order of removal comes from final_order_before_apprehension.

How an arrest happened comes from apprehension_method_simple. We treated "Custodial Arrest," which includes ICE's Criminal Alien Program, and "287(g) Program" as custodial arrests, meaning a jail or prison handed the person to ICE. All other arrests, mostly those ICE codes "At-Large Arrest," are non-custodial. Arrests at ICE's two Baltimore offices were identified from event_landmark, where ICE lists them as "NON DETAINED ENCOUNTERS AT BAL ERO" and "BALTIMORE ISAP OFFICE."

The story uses two time periods. Year-over-year comparisons use Jan. 1 through July 31 of each year, because the records end Aug. 6. Everything else covers Jan. 20, 2025, when President Donald Trump's second term began, through Aug. 6, 2026, unless the text says otherwise. Comparisons around Maryland's 287(g) ban are monthly averages over periods of different lengths, Jan. 20, 2025, through Feb. 16, 2026 (393 days), and Feb. 17 through Aug. 6, 2026 (171 days): each count was divided by the number of days and multiplied by 30.4375, the average length of a month. National figures come from the same file, using all states. The state comparison includes the 43 states and territories with at least 1,000 arrests since Jan. 20, 2025, and leaves out the District of Columbia.

The chart comparing Maryland with the nation divides, for each month from January 2024 through July 2026, arrests coded "Other Immigration Violator" by all arrests, in Maryland and in all states.

Places

To place arrests in counties, we first checked whether event_landmark names a county or Baltimore City. If it did not, we used the city ICE recorded in apprehension_city_original, matched to a county through a lookup table CNS built from every spelling in the data. Most spellings matched a ZIP code directory directly; others were corrected by hand or matched to the closest valid spelling, and a few could not be placed. In all, 98.7% of arrests, and 99.5% of those since Jan. 20, 2025, were assigned a county. Arrest rates use 2020 census populations from the Maryland Department of Planning and leave out arrests at ICE's Baltimore offices, which people come to from across the state. The Eastern Shore includes Caroline, Cecil, Dorchester, Kent, Queen Anne's, Somerset, Talbot, Wicomico and Worcester counties. Map circles are placed at the center of each city.

What the records can't tell us

The records show each person's criminal history and how an arrest happened, not why ICE made the arrest. "No record" reflects what ICE's systems showed at the time of arrest, which can be incomplete. The records do not show immigration status. ICE often records a general area rather than an address, so CNS placed arrests by the city or county ICE listed. ICE keeps updating its records, so counts for the most recent months may grow.
