ICE's arrests in Maryland nearly doubled in 2026. The growth was driven by people with no criminal record

Since President Donald Trump took office, about 63% of ICE’s arrests in Maryland have been of people with no criminal record, compared with about 39% nationwide, agency records show.

By Mehedi Marof · Capital News Service

Scroll to read↓

From January through July of 2026, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement made nearly 3,000 arrests in Maryland of people who had neither a criminal conviction nor a pending criminal charge. That is more such arrests than the agency made in the state in all of 2025.

Total ICE arrests in Maryland nearly doubled, from 2,224 in January through July 2025 to over 4,000 in the same months of 2026. People without a record accounted for the entire increase. Arrests of people with convictions and of people facing pending charges each fell slightly.

"Maryland feels like a microcosm of what's happening nationally," said Yulie Landan, a staff attorney at the National Immigration Project, a legal group suing ICE over its arrests in the state and over conditions in its Baltimore hold room. "We've been seeing massive increases in arrests there."

Capital News Service analyzed ICE arrest, detainer and detention records that the Deportation Data Project, a research group at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA, obtained through Freedom of Information Act lawsuits. The records run through Aug. 6, 2026.

Maureen Sweeney, who directs the Immigration Clinic at the University of Maryland Francis King Carey School of Law, said she sees "a big disconnect between the administration's rhetoric about ... keeping the country safe from dangerous people and what's actually happening."

What is happening, she said, is that ICE is “targeting and rounding up anybody who's a non-citizen… especially people who are brown or Black and look different from white Americans."

What "no record" means

ICE sorts the people it arrests by their criminal history at the time of arrest: people previously convicted of a crime, people facing a pending criminal charge and people with neither. ICE calls the third group “other immigration violators.” They are accused of violating civil immigration law, such as entering the country without permission or overstaying a visa.

Arrests rose across the country too, but followed different patterns. Nationwide, ICE made nearly 253,000 arrests from January through July, up about 75% from a year earlier.

Arrests of people with no record grew the most nationally as well. But arrests of people with pending charges also rose by nearly two-thirds, and arrests of people with convictions by about a fifth. In Maryland, both of those groups shrank.

ICE makes arrests in two main ways. In a custodial arrest, a jail, prison or other agency hands someone over to ICE. In a non-custodial arrest, agents find the person at home, at work, on the street or at an ICE office.

In Maryland, the increase came from non-custodial arrests, which more than doubled, from nearly 1,600 in the first seven months of 2025 to about 3,700 in the same months of 2026. Custodial arrests dipped from 626 to 515. Nationwide, both rose about 75%.

ICE’s arrests were most concentrated on the Eastern Shore. Since Trump took office, about 23% of the agency’s arrests in Maryland have been in the Eastern Shore’s nine counties, a region home to about 7% of the state’s residents.

Arrests of people with no record there nearly quadrupled. ICE made 730 such arrests on the Eastern Shore from January through July, up from 189 in the same months of 2025.

The five Maryland counties with the highest ICE arrest rates since Jan. 20, 2025, are all on the Eastern Shore: Wicomico, Worcester, Talbot, Dorchester and Somerset.

Wicomico, which includes Salisbury, had the highest, about 107 arrests for every 10,000 residents, nearly all of them non-custodial.

ICE also made arrests inside its own offices in Baltimore, including one where immigrants under supervision report for scheduled check-ins. Since Jan. 20, 2025, the agency has recorded 889 arrests at the two Baltimore offices, about three in four of them of people with no criminal record. Those arrests rose from 62 in the first three months of 2025 to 281 in the first three months of 2026, the most in any quarter in the records.

They have continued since the records end. On Nov. 5, Maria Flores Garcia, a University of Maryland freshman, and her mother were detained when they went to the mother's annual check-in at ICE's Baltimore office. They were then moved to Virginia and on to Louisiana, according to her boyfriend and Prince George's County Executive Aisha N. Braveboy.

Their path is common for people ICE detains in Maryland. Of the roughly 8,300 people the agency detained after arrests in the state from Jan. 20, 2025, through early August 2026, about nine in 10 spent most of their detention outside Maryland, and about two in three were held in three or more facilities. More than 6,200 were held at some point in Louisiana, Texas or Mississippi, more than 1,000 miles from Baltimore.

"Maryland was really kind of on the forefront of mass increases in arrests, whether it be in the street or in check-ins," Landan said.

"They keep saying we're going after the worst of the worst and the reality is they're going after everyone," she said. "It's just about the numbers for them."

Maryland stands out

Since Trump took office, about 63% of ICE's arrests in Maryland have been of people with no criminal record, compared with about 39% nationwide.

Among the 43 states and territories where ICE made at least 1,000 arrests in that period, only New York and West Virginia had a higher share.

The share has swung widely over the years. In late 2022, under President Joe Biden, about three in four people ICE arrested in Maryland had no record. By December 2024, the share had fallen to about 17%.

In January 2025, the month Trump returned to office, Maryland's share matched the nation's, at about 21%. Every month since, it has been higher than the national share, peaking at about 77% in February 2026, double the national rate that month.

How we did this analysis

Capital News Service analyzed U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement arrest records that the Deportation Data Project, a research group at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA, obtained through Freedom of Information Act lawsuits. The records cover Oct. 1, 2022, through Aug. 6, 2026. We removed rows the project flags as likely duplicates, leaving 12,641 arrests in Maryland, 9,071 of them since Jan. 20, 2025.

Year-over-year comparisons use January through July of each year, because the records end in early August. All other figures cover Jan. 20, 2025, when President Donald Trump's second term began, through Aug. 6, 2026. Monthly charts leave out August 2026 because the month is incomplete. Some people were arrested more than once, so the story counts arrests, not people.

We counted arrests ICE coded as custodial, including its Criminal Alien Program, and arrests under 287(g) agreements as custodial. All others are non-custodial. Arrests inside ICE's Baltimore field office and its check-in office for people under supervision are identified by the location ICE recorded; there were 889.

We assigned arrests to counties using the city or area ICE recorded. About 5% of arrests, 495, had no usable location and are left off the map. County rates use 2020 census populations and are per resident, not per immigrant resident. The state comparison includes the 43 states and territories with at least 1,000 arrests since Jan. 20, 2025; the District of Columbia is left out.

What the records can't tell us

The records show who ICE arrested and how, not why. "No record" reflects only what ICE's systems showed at the time of arrest, which can be incomplete. The records do not show a person's immigration status or where they lived. ICE keeps updating its records, so counts for recent months may grow.
